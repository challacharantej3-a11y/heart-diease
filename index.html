Getting the right kind of help, at the right time

Not every concern needs an ambulance, and not every concern can wait for a routine appointment. Here's how to tell the difference, plus where this site's information comes from.

Having symptoms right now?

Chest pain or pressure, shortness of breath, or pain spreading to your arm, jaw, or back can signal a heart attack in progress. Call your local emergency number immediately — don't wait, and don't drive yourself. See the full list on the warning signs page.

Choosing where to go

Call emergency services if:

  • You have chest pain, pressure, or discomfort that's new, severe, or won't go away
  • You have sudden shortness of breath, cold sweat, or lightheadedness
  • Symptoms are combined with pain in the arm, jaw, neck, or back
  • You're simply unsure but something feels seriously wrong

Book a doctor's visit if:

  • You want to check your blood pressure, cholesterol, or blood sugar
  • You have a family history and want to understand your own risk
  • You're noticing mild, gradual changes like fatigue or reduced stamina
  • You want a plan for diet, activity, smoking, or other habits

Finding a doctor or specialist

A primary care doctor is usually the right first stop for general concerns, routine screening, or questions about risk factors. They can refer you to a cardiologist (a heart specialist) if further evaluation is needed. If you don't currently have a primary care provider, your health insurance plan's directory, a local community health center, or a nearby hospital system's website are typically the fastest ways to find one accepting new patients.

Supporting a loved one

If you're a caregiver, the same rules apply on their behalf: act on emergency symptoms immediately, and use routine visits to stay ahead of the factors that can be managed. It's also worth learning the symptoms that look different in women, covered on the warning signs page, since they're the ones most often second-guessed or dismissed.

Our sources

Every statistic and recommendation on this site is drawn from established public health organizations. Where information reflects general guidance rather than a hard statistic, we've tried to say so plainly.

  • Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) — U.S. heart disease statistics, risk factor definitions, and leading cause of death data.
  • American Heart Association (AHA) — heart attack warning signs, symptom differences by sex, physical activity guidelines, and smoking cessation data.
  • World Health Organization (WHO) — global cardiovascular disease context and mortality figures.

This site summarizes and paraphrases publicly available guidance from the organizations above; it does not reproduce their materials directly. For complete, current detail, visit the linked sources.
